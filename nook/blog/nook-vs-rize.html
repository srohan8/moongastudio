Nook vs Rize.io: two different tools for ADHD focus

Nook and Rize.io are both recommended for people with ADHD who struggle to focus — but they solve fundamentally different problems. Rize tracks where your time actually goes. Nook gives you the ambient co-presence of body doubling so you can start in the first place. Understanding the difference is the key to using both effectively.

When people search for ADHD focus tools, Nook and Rize often appear in the same lists. That makes sense — both are calm, low-distraction apps designed to help you do better work. But they target completely different parts of the ADHD focus problem.

Nook

Helps you start and sustain focus

Uses ambient co-presence (body doubling) and Pomodoro rhythms so you can activate focus and stay in it — especially when working alone feels impossible.

Rize

Helps you understand your time

Automatically tracks how you spend time on your computer — what apps, what tasks, what categories — and surfaces patterns so you can improve your habits over time.

Put simply: Rize tells you where your time went. Nook helps you protect time in the moment. For many people with ADHD, both are useful — at different points in the day.

What is Rize.io?

Rize is an AI-powered time tracker for Mac and Windows. It runs in the background and automatically categorises how you spend time across apps and tasks — coding, writing, meetings, social media — without requiring you to manually start and stop timers. At the end of the day or week, it shows you a detailed breakdown of where your time actually went versus where you intended it to go.

Rize also includes focus sessions with a built-in timer, focus music integration, and break reminders. For ADHD users, the most valuable feature is often the honest data: many people are surprised to discover how much time disappears into unfocused switching between apps, and seeing that clearly can motivate change.

What is Nook?

Nook is a virtual co-working app. You join a named room — Beach Café, The Cascade, The Meadow, or After Hours — set a brief intention, and work alongside whoever else is there. No camera. No scheduling. No sign-up required. The room runs a shared Pomodoro timer, ambient sound plays quietly in the background, and you can see other people working beside you.

Nook works through body doubling — the well-documented ADHD strategy where the presence of another person (even a stranger, even online) activates focus that working alone can't produce. For many people with ADHD, the single most effective thing they can do is stop working alone.

Which solves the ADHD focus problem better?

The honest answer is that they solve different ADHD focus problems — and which one matters more depends on where you're stuck.

If your problem is activation — you can't start

This is the classic ADHD executive dysfunction challenge. You know exactly what you need to do. You just cannot make yourself begin. Time tracking won't help here — knowing where your time went yesterday doesn't help you open the document right now.

This is where Nook's body doubling works directly. The presence of other people in a room creates the external structure that compensates for reduced internal regulation. Drop into a room, set your intention ("writing the report"), and the social context makes starting easier. Many users describe this as the only thing that reliably works when nothing else does.

If your problem is awareness — time disappears without you noticing

ADHD time blindness is real: hours pass without a sense of how much time has gone, tasks take far longer than expected, and the day ends without the work you planned getting done. This is where Rize's passive tracking is genuinely useful — it creates an honest record that makes the invisible visible.

Seeing that you spent 3 hours on email when you meant to spend 30 minutes, or that your "focus time" was actually 45 minutes of real work across a 4-hour stretch, is the kind of data that can motivate genuine change in how you structure your day.

If your problem is both

Use both. They don't conflict — Nook runs in a browser tab or on your phone while Rize runs in the background on your desktop. You can be in a Nook room (body doubling, Pomodoro rhythm, ambient sound) while Rize is quietly tracking what you're actually working on. At the end of the day, Rize tells you whether you succeeded; Nook helped you try.

"I use Nook to actually get into work, and Rize to see if I did."

Should I use Nook or Rize.io?

Start with Nook if…

  • You struggle to start tasks at all
  • Working alone feels impossible or draining
  • You want free, zero-friction body doubling
  • You're on Android (Rize is desktop-only)
  • You want ambient sound alongside your work
  • You don't want another subscription

Add Rize if…

  • You lose track of where time goes
  • You want honest data on your work patterns
  • You work on Mac or Windows desktop
  • You want structured focus sessions with analytics
  • Insights and trends help you build habits

Nook or Rize: which is right for you?

Nook and Rize address different stages of the ADHD focus problem. Rize is a diagnostic tool — it shows you the patterns that are costing you time. Nook is an activation tool — it puts you in a room with other people so you can actually do the work.

If you're choosing only one: start with Nook. It's free, requires no account, and the body doubling effect is immediate — you feel it the first time you join a room. If activation isn't your bottleneck but time awareness is, add Rize on top.

For ADHD body doubling specifically — the co-presence of real people working alongside you — Nook is the dedicated tool. Rize doesn't offer that; it's a powerful complement, not a replacement.

Nook is free, requires no account, and works on web and Android. Join a room and experience body doubling for yourself — in under 10 seconds.
