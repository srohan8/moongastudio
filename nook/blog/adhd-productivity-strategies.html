5 productivity strategies that actually work for ADHD (2026)

Most productivity advice is built for neurotypical brains. These five strategies are different — they work with how an ADHD brain actually functions, not against it.

ADHD isn't a motivation problem. It's a regulation problem. The standard advice — make a list, set a goal, just focus — fails because it assumes you can access focus on demand. Most people with ADHD can't. What they can do is create conditions where focus becomes more likely.

These five strategies do exactly that. They're backed by research and, more importantly, by the real experience of people who've tried everything else first.

01

Body doubling — work alongside someone else

Body doubling is the single most consistently effective ADHD focus strategy. The idea is simple: you work in the presence of another person. Not with them — alongside them. They don't need to help, check in, or even notice you. Their presence alone is enough to regulate your nervous system and make starting easier.

The research supports this. A 2021 study in Frontiers in Psychology found that the mere presence of others significantly improved task performance in people with ADHD — an effect that doesn't appear at the same level in neurotypical subjects.

In practice, body doubling works in coffee shops, libraries, and co-working spaces. But those options require leaving the house, spending money, and finding somewhere that's actually quiet enough. "I just needed to feel like someone else was in the room."

That's the most common thing people say when they describe why Nook works for them. It's not accountability. It's presence.

02

Pomodoro timers — make time visible and bounded

ADHD time blindness is real. Without external structure, time collapses — hours disappear or a 10-minute task expands to fill an afternoon. The Pomodoro technique addresses this by creating visible, bounded work units: 25 minutes of work, 5-minute break. Or 50 and 10, for people who hit flow and need longer stretches.

The key isn't the specific ratio — it's that the timer creates a horizon. You're not working until you're done (overwhelming, unbounded). You're working until the timer goes off (concrete, manageable). The break is built in, so there's no guilt about stopping.

The catch: setting up yet another app, configuring timers, and remembering to start them adds friction that ADHD brains hate. Tools that build the timer into the environment — the way Nook does per room — remove that friction entirely.

03

Environment design — remove decisions before you start

Every decision you make before starting work costs activation energy. Where will I sit? What will I listen to? Is my phone nearby? That energy is finite, and for ADHD brains it's often spent before any actual work begins.

The fix is environment design: making the start of a work session require as few decisions as possible. Practically, this means:

  • A dedicated workspace — even a chair you only sit in when working
  • Ambient sound already queued and ready (not chosen fresh each time)
  • Phone physically out of reach, not just face-down
  • A consistent ritual — the same sequence of actions that signal "work begins now"

The ritual matters more than the space. If your ritual is "open Nook, pick a room, type my intention" — that three-step sequence becomes the trigger. Your brain learns that those three actions mean focus is coming.

04

Intention setting — name what you're doing before you do it

Vague tasks are dangerous for ADHD brains. "Work on the project" could mean anything, which means it effectively means nothing — and you'll spend twenty minutes deciding where to start. Specific tasks are different: "write the introduction of section 3" has a clear start and a visible end.

Before you begin any work session, write down one sentence: I am working on [specific thing] until [specific point]. Not a to-do list. One sentence. The specificity is the point.

This is why Nook asks for an intention when you enter a room — not to share with others (though others can see it), but to force you to name what you're doing. The act of writing it down is the commitment.

05

Brain dumping — clear the queue before you start

ADHD working memory is unreliable. Unfinished thoughts, stray tasks, and ambient worries compete for attention during any work session. The solution isn't to ignore them — it's to park them.

A brain dump is a timed, unstructured capture session: set a timer for five minutes, write down everything on your mind, don't organise it, don't judge it. The goal is to move things out of active memory and into a place you trust. Once something is written down, your brain stops cycling back to remember it.

Some people use a notebook. Some use a voice memo. The format matters less than the habit. The critical piece is that you do it before you try to focus — not during, when the interruption itself is the problem.

What these strategies share

None of these strategies work by increasing willpower. They all work by changing the conditions in which you're trying to focus — reducing friction, creating structure, or harnessing the regulation that comes from external presence.

That's the common thread in effective ADHD productivity tools: they don't demand that your brain work differently. They reshape the environment until your brain can do what it's already capable of.
